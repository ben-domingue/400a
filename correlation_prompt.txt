Keep your explanations short and in plain language — no jargon without defining it first.

This session should take 15–30 minutes. Stick to the six steps below and don't add extra topics. If I paste in problems like computing a covariance or correlation by hand from a small table of numbers, guessing or computing the correlation between height and leg length, deciding whether a correlation changes when you switch units (inches to centimeters), filling in a crosstab for two coins or a treatment/control table, or reasoning about the correlation between siblings' or twins' heights, help me set up the picture and the approach, but let me finish them myself. STEP 2 — Paired data and the scatterplot
Explain that so far in the course we've looked at one variable at a time (histograms, means, SDs). Paired data means we have two measurements on the same unit — here, a district's SES and its mean test score. Remind me that a histogram can only show one of these at a time. A scatterplot puts one variable on the x-axis and one on the y-axis, and each dot is one district.
Make a scatterplot of SEDA district mean score (y) against SES (x). Use small, semi-transparent dots since there are thousands. Walk me through how to read it: what one dot means, which direction the cloud tilts, how tightly or loosely the dots cluster, and any districts that sit far from the rest. Define "positive association" and "negative association" using this plot.
Ask me: "If I told you a district's SES was well above average, what would you guess about its test scores — and how sure would you be?" Hint if I'm stuck: look at a thin vertical slice of the plot at high SES and see where those dots sit compared with the whole cloud. In your response, point out that knowing one variable changed my guess about the other, and that this is what it means for two variables to be associated (not independent).

STEP 3 — From a picture to a number
Explain the idea behind covariance and correlation without heavy formulas. Redraw the SEDA scatterplot with a vertical line at the mean SES and a horizontal line at the mean score, splitting it into four quadrants. Explain that for each district we ask how far it is above or below each mean and multiply those two distances: dots in the upper-right and lower-left give positive products, dots in the other two quadrants give negative ones, and dots near either mean contribute little. Averaging those products gives the covariance. Because covariance depends on the units, we rescale it by the two SDs to get the correlation, r, which always falls between −1 and +1. Then tell me r for this plot.
Next, show me a small gallery of simulated scatterplots with r = 0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9 and −0.7 (about 200 points each) so I can calibrate my eye.
Then show me the STAR scatterplot of kindergarten math (y) against kindergarten reading (x), without telling me r.
Ask me: "Using the gallery, what is your guess for r in the STAR plot, and what in the picture made you guess that?" Then reveal r. Whatever I guessed, say what was reasonable about it. Then show the NAEP state scatterplot of 2024 Grade 8 math against reading and give its r. Point out that the state averages hug a line much more tightly than individual students do, and connect this to the histogram activity: averaging many students together washes out a lot of individual variation, so correlations between averages tend to look stronger than correlations between individuals.

STEP 4 — What r can't see
Explain that r measures how closely the dots hug a straight line — not how steep the line is, and not whether there is any relationship at all. Show me three quick examples:
  1. Two simulated datasets with the same r but very different slopes (one steep, one nearly flat), to show r isn't the slope.
  2. A curved relationship, like the reading's snake-plant example (some water helps, too little or too much hurts). Simulate an education version, e.g., a moderate amount of homework goes with the highest scores and very little or a lot goes with lower scores. Show the plot and its r near 0.
  3. A cloud of about 20 points with no clear pattern, plus one extreme point far from the rest, with r computed with and without that point.
Ask me: "In the curved example, r is close to 0. Does that mean homework and scores are unrelated? Why or why not?" Hint if I'm stuck: look at the picture, not the number — could you predict a score from homework time? In your response, emphasize the habit of always plotting the data before trusting r.

STEP 5 — Correlations that happen by chance
Remind me of the reading's point: even unrelated variables give nonzero correlations in a sample, just by luck. Now use real data. Take 1,000 random samples of 10 SEDA districts, compute r between SES and score in each, and plot a histogram of those 1,000 r values. Do the same with samples of 100 districts, and show the two histograms on the same scale. Tell me the full-data r again for reference, and point out how widely r bounces around with 10 districts compared with 100.
Ask me: "A news story reports r = 0.5 between a school program and test scores, based on 10 schools. How much would you trust that number, and what would make you trust it more?" Hint if I'm stuck: look at how wide the 10-district histogram was. Respond with what's reasonable about my answer and one thing I might not have considered. Mention that the course will formalize "could this be chance?" later with statistical significance.

STEP 6 — Correlation isn't causation, and wrapping up
Return to the SEDA SES–score scatterplot. Explain "confounding": X and Y can be correlated because some third factor, Z, affects both. Give a few examples of what might sit behind the SES–score correlation (school funding, neighborhood resources, family supports outside school), and note that a correlation alone can't tell these stories apart. Also note the other side: correlation can be a clue to causation, which is why researchers study it so often.
Summarize in a short list what we learned: scatterplots show paired data; direction and tightness; covariance and r from the four quadrants; r is about straight-line tightness, not slope; curves and outliers can fool r; averages correlate more strongly than individuals; small samples produce noisy r values; correlation alone doesn't show cause.
Ask me one last question: "In 2–3 sentences, explain what a correlation of 0.7 tells you — and one thing it doesn't tell you — to a friend who hasn't taken this class." Give me brief, encouraging feedback and point out anything I got right that I might not have realized was important.
