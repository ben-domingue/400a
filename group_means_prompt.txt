Keep your explanations short and in plain language — no jargon without defining it first.

This session should take 15–30 minutes. Stick to the five steps below and don't add extra topics. STEP 2 — Comparing two groups
Explain that we have paired data again, as with scatterplots, but now one of the two variables is a group (small or regular class) rather than a number. Make overlaid, semi-transparent density plots (or side-by-side histograms on the same axis) of kindergarten math scores for small and regular classes, and mark each group's mean with a vertical line. Walk me through what I'm seeing: the two shapes overlap almost completely, plenty of regular-class students outscore plenty of small-class students, and yet the small-class mean is a bit higher. Tell me the two means, their difference in points, and that difference in SD units (difference divided by the overall SD of math scores; it should be about 0.15–0.2). For a sense of scale, mention the video's example: a 0.09 SD effect of universal free breakfast on math is about 1.6 months of learning.
Ask me: "If I told you a kindergartner was in a small class, would that change your guess about their math score? By how much, and how sure would you be?" Hint if I'm stuck: compare where the two mean lines sit, then look at how wide each curve is. In your response, connect this to the correlation activity: if knowing the group shifts what we expect for the score, the two variables are associated (not independent), even when the individual distributions overlap heavily.

STEP 3 — The thought experiment: samples of 10
Explain the setup: for this exercise, pretend the roughly 3,800 STAR students are the whole population. In real research we never see the population. We see one sample and use its average to estimate the population's average. Draw a random sample of 10 students from the small classes and 10 from the regular classes (with replacement), compute each sample's mean, and plot them as labeled points ("S" for small, "R" for regular) on a horizontal number line that also shows the two true population means. Then repeat this 19 more times, adding each new pair to the plot, so there are 20 S's and 20 R's. Stress that in a real study we would only ever get one of these pairs.
Ask me: "In how many of the 20 pairs did the small-class sample come out ahead? If you had run only one of these studies, what might you have concluded about small classes?" Hint if I'm stuck: count the pairs where S lands to the left of R. Respond with what's reasonable about my answer and one thing I might not have considered.

STEP 4 — Sampling distributions and sample size
Now take the thought experiment further. Repeat the draw 1,000 times with 10 students per group and plot the density of the 1,000 small-class means and the 1,000 regular-class means. Do the same with 100 per group and with 1,000 per group (sampling with replacement). Show all three panels stacked, on the same x-axis. Under each panel, report the share of the 1,000 studies where the small-class mean beat the regular-class mean (roughly two-thirds at n = 10, around 90% at n = 100 and nearly always at n = 1,000).
Define two terms using these pictures. The population distribution of outcomes is the spread of individual students' scores, which is what we plotted in Step 2. The sampling distribution of the mean is the spread of sample means across many repeated samples, which is what these new curves show. Point out that the true difference between the groups never changes; only the width of the sampling distributions does. Say that "statistical power" is our ability to detect a real difference, and that it grows as samples get larger. Connect this to the correlation activity, where r bounced around much more with 10 districts than with 100.
Ask me: "A principal wants to try small classes in one school and compare 10 students in a small class with 10 in a regular class. Based on these pictures, what would you tell them?" Hint if I'm stuck: look at the n = 10 panel and ask how often a study that size would even show small classes ahead. Respond with what's reasonable about my answer and one thing I might not have considered. Mention that the course will formalize these ideas soon, when we get to statistical inference.

STEP 5 — Wrapping up
Summarize in a short list what we learned: comparing groups with a density or histogram for each; summarizing the comparison as a difference in means, in points or in SD units; association means knowing the group changes what we expect; a sample mean is an estimate of a population mean; sample means bounce around from sample to sample; with small samples, even a real difference is hard to see; larger samples shrink the sampling distribution and give more power.
