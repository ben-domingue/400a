STEP 2 — From a cloud of dots to a line
Explain that correlation treated the two variables the same way; it didn't matter which was "first." Regression gives them different jobs. The independent variable goes on the x-axis and is often the one we think might be a cause (here, SES). The dependent variable goes on the y-axis and is often the one we think might be an effect (here, test scores). Give me a 30-second refresher on lines: in algebra we write y = mx + b, where b is where the line crosses the y-axis and m is the slope (rise over run). In regression the same line is usually written y = a + bx, so a is now the intercept and b is the slope.
Make the SEDA scatterplot of mean score (y) against SES (x), with small, semi-transparent dots. Then split the districts into 10 equal-sized groups by SES and plot the average score of each group as a large, bold point on top of the cloud. Point out that the 10 averages climb in close to a straight line. Explain that this is what regression is after: for each value of x, what do we expect y to be? Statisticians call this a "conditional expectation," the average of y among units with a given x. Then add the regression line and show that it runs right through those 10 averages.
Ask me: "Using the plot, what score would you expect for a district with SES of about 1? And would every district with SES of 1 have that score?" Hint if I'm stuck: find 1 on the x-axis, go up to the line, then look at how spread out the dots are above and below that spot. In your response, stress that the line gives an expected value, not a guarantee for any single district.

STEP 3 — What makes a line the "best" one
Explain that many lines could be drawn through this cloud, so we need a rule for picking one. For any line, each district misses by some vertical distance (actual score minus the line's predicted score); this miss is called a residual or error. Draw three lines on the scatterplot, each in its own panel: a flat line at the average score (that is, ignore SES and guess the average for everyone), a line that is too steep (slope of about 0.6), and the regression line. In each panel, show a random sample of about 30 districts with thin vertical segments from each dot to the line, so I can see the misses. Under each panel, report the sum of squared errors (SSE) over all districts: square each residual, then add them up. The regression line should have the smallest SSE, well under half that of the flat line. Explain that ordinary least squares (OLS) regression means picking the line, out of all possible lines, with the smallest SSE.
Ask me: "Why do you think we square the misses before adding them up, instead of just adding them?" Hint if I'm stuck: some dots are above the line and some are below — what happens when you add a +0.3 and a −0.3? Respond with what's reasonable about my answer and one thing I might not have considered (for example, squaring also makes big misses count much more than small ones). Then mention that 1 minus (SSE of the regression line divided by SSE of the flat line) is r squared, about 0.59 here, which means SES accounts for about 59% of the variation in district scores. Connect this to the correlation activity: r squared is just r times r (0.77 × 0.77).

STEP 4 — Reading the slope and the intercept
Tell me the fitted equation, which should be close to: predicted score = −0.07 + 0.30 × SES. Walk me through each piece:
  1. The slope, 0.30, is the expected change in score for a one-unit increase in SES. Two districts that differ by 1 on SES are expected to differ by about 0.3 student SDs in scores.
  2. The intercept, −0.07, is the expected score when SES is 0. Here SES of 0 is roughly the national average, so the intercept is meaningful: an average-SES district is expected to score a little below the national average. Note that the intercept isn't always useful: if x were a district's enrollment, x = 0 (a district with no students) would make no sense.
  3. The slope comes straight from things we already know: slope = r × (SD of y) / (SD of x). Show me the arithmetic with the actual values (about 0.77 × 0.34 / 0.87 ≈ 0.30). Also point out that the line always passes through the point (mean of x, mean of y), which is how the intercept is found once we know the slope.
Then give me a quick warning about extrapolation: SES in these data runs from about −4.6 to 2.9, so the line tells us little about a district with SES of 5.
Ask me: "A school board member reads that the slope is 0.30 and says, 'So if we raise our district's SES by one unit, our scores will go up by 0.3.' What would you tell them?" Hint if I'm stuck: think back to the confounding discussion from the correlation activity. Respond with what's reasonable about my answer and one thing I might not have considered. Note that regression has a direction (x predicts y), which is a reason to use it when we're interested in causes, but the line by itself doesn't prove that x causes y.

STEP 5 — When x is just 0 or 1
Explain that x doesn't have to be a continuous number. Make a new variable that is 1 if a majority of the district's students are in rural schools (rural greater than 0.5) and 0 otherwise; about half the districts should be rural. Make a scatterplot of score (y) against this 0/1 variable (x), with the dots jittered sideways so they don't all stack on top of each other. Mark each group's mean score and draw the regression line, which connects the two group means.
Ask me: "The line goes from the non-rural mean at x = 0 to the rural mean at x = 1. What do you think the slope of this line tells us?" Hint if I'm stuck: the line moves one step to the right (from 0 to 1) — how far does it go up or down? After I answer, show that the slope (about −0.075) is exactly the difference in group means (rural minus non-rural) and that the intercept is the non-rural mean. Connect this to the group means activity: if x were treatment (1) versus control (0) in an experiment like STAR, the regression slope would be the difference in means, which is the estimated effect of treatment. Mention that the course will build on this when we analyze experiments.

STEP 6 — Wrapping up
Summarize in a short list what we learned: regression gives x and y different jobs (independent and dependent variables); the regression line traces what we expect y to be for each x; residuals are vertical misses; OLS picks the line with the smallest sum of squared errors; r squared compares that line with just guessing the average; the slope is the expected change in y per one-unit change in x, and equals r × SD(y) / SD(x); the intercept is the expected y when x = 0, which may or may not be meaningful; don't extrapolate beyond the data; with a 0/1 x, the slope is a difference in means.
